Context and assumptions

Design superelevation rate (305-4.01, 305-4.02)

Open-roadway conditions (V ≥ 50 mph, rural and high-speed urban) use AASHTO Method 5 and Figures 305-4B/C/D for emax = 4, 6 or 8%. Low-speed urban streets (V ≤ 45 mph, except interchange ramps) use AASHTO Method 2 and Figure 305-4E. Each table lists the minimum radius for each rate; for a radius between rows the next higher rate is the conservative choice, or interpolate (the IDM examples 305-4.1 to 4.3 interpolate in Figure 305-4E). NC = normal crown (−2.0%); RC = remove adverse crown (+2.0%, planar at the normal cross slope).

Consider a design speed 5 mph higher (grade 3 to 5%) or 10 mph higher (grade over 5%) for the minimum radius at the bottom of a downgrade, and up to 10 mph higher at the end of a long tangent (305-4.01(03)).

Axis of rotation (305-4.03)

Undivided highways are generally rotated about the centerline or roadway crown, which gives the least elevation difference between the pavement edges and their normal profiles. Rotating about the inside or outside edge of the traveled way is acceptable to meet field conditions such as curb drainage or roadside development. On a two-lane road with an auxiliary lane, rotate about the centerline of the two through lanes.

When rotating about an edge, the whole traveled way rotates as a plane once the adverse crown is removed, so n1 in Equation 305-4.1 is the total number of lanes (twice the lanes on each side), and the runoff and tangent runout are correspondingly longer. The page keeps the axis at its normal-crown elevation; the centerline rises (inside-edge rotation) or drops (outside-edge rotation) relative to its normal profile.

Divided highways (305-4.03(02)): medians under 15 ft rotate about the centerline of the whole section; medians 15 to 60 ft rotate each roadway about its median edge; medians of 60 ft or more may use the centerline or crown of each roadway or the median edge; with concrete median barrier, rotate about the barrier edges. For a one-way roadway crowned at its own centerline and rotated about the median edge, use "inside edge" for the roadway on the inside of the curve and "outside edge" for the roadway on the outside. Where the cross slope is not constant across the lanes (e.g., a crowned section rotated about the median edge), Equation 305-4.1 can overstate the runoff and the IDM calls for incremental calculations (305-4.04(01) item 2; Equation 305-4.3 for tangent runout).

Superelevation runoff (305-4.04(01))

Lr = (w · n1 · e · bw) / G    (Equation 305-4.1), rounded up to the next 5 ft
  • w = width of one lane, 12 ft minimum (use 12 ft even if lanes are narrower; wider lanes increase Lr in proportion).
  • G = maximum relative gradient, Figure 305-4F: 0.78% at 15 mph to 0.50% at 50 mph and above.
  • bw = 1.0 for all routes on the State highway system; reduced factors (Figure 305-4F) only off-system or where physical constraints prevent the full length.
  • For divided highways rotated about the median edges, Equation 305-4.1 may overstate the length; use incremental calculations (305-4.04(01) item 2).

Tangent runout (305-4.04(02))

TR = Lr · Snormal / e    (Equation 305-4.2)

For a divided highway where the cross slope is not constant across the lanes, Equation 305-4.3 applies: TR = Snormal · nRC · w / G.

Placing the runoff (305-4.04(03))

Simple curve: 2/3 of Lr on the tangent and 1/3 on the curve. On two-lane, two-way roads it may be adjusted down to 50% on the tangent. For multilane roads apply bw instead of changing the distribution. Reverse curves: apply the simple-curve distribution to each curve, with at least 300 ft between PT and PC for a continuously rotating plane.

High-side shoulder (305-4.06(01))

Paved shoulder ≤ 4 ft (or up to 6 ft for barrier offset): same plane as the travel lane. Wider shoulders: 4% normal slope when e ≤ 4%; 2% down away from the traveled way when 4% < e ≤ 6%; 1% toward the traveled way when e > 6%. Rollover between the shoulder and travel lane must not exceed 8%.

This page computes Lr from Equation 305-4.1 and shows the Figure 305-4G value beside it. Figure 305-4G as printed disagrees with Equation 305-4.1 (using the Figure 305-4F relative gradients and rounding up to 5 ft) in about a quarter of its cells, usually by 5 ft; a few entries are clearly misprinted (e = 6.0% at 40 mph shows 160 ft where the equation gives 125 ft; e = 7.6% at 30 mph shows 170 ft for 140 ft; e = 3.2% at 25 mph shows 45 ft for 55 ft). The equation value is used as the design value; where the table differs, the page flags it so you can choose.
